Assignment

Q1. Why is a plain SHA-256 hash a bad password store?

Fast: billions of guesses per second on a GPU; the offline attack cannot be rate-limited; zero memory cost, so attack hardware is cheap. Cite Theory §1 and Demo 3.

Q2. What does the salt defend against — and what does it NOT defend against?

Defeats precomputed/rainbow tables and cross-user amortisation (Demo 1 Table B). Does NOT stop a targeted brute-force of one account — that is memory-hardness's job (Theory §5).

Q3. Decode the PHC string $argon2id$v=19$m=19456,t=2,p=1$... field by field.

argon2id variant - version 1.3 (0x13) - m=19456 KiB = 19 MiB - t=2 passes - p=1 lane - then unpadded base64 salt and tag (Theory §3).

Q4. Argon2d vs Argon2i vs Argon2id — one use case each.

d: data-dependent, max GPU resistance, side-channel unsafe (crypto/PoW). i: data-independent, side-channel safe. id: hybrid, recommended for password storage (Theory §2.2).

Q5. Login must stay under 200 ms. m=19456,t=2 measured 60 ms; you have 8 GB spare RAM. Can you raise m, and to what?

Time scales ~linearly with m (Demo 2): 3x head-room, so m about 48 MiB keeps you near 180 ms while tripling attacker RAM cost. Also budget for concurrency: 100 logins x m KiB (Theory §4).

Q6. Why does increasing t help less than increasing m, per unit of user time?

t multiplies CPU time only — GPUs have throughput to burn. m forces the attacker to buy RAM per parallel guess; RAM, not logic, dominates die cost (Theory §2/§4, Demo 3).

Q7. From Demo 3: how many guesses/sec would 10,000 GPUs do at your measured PBKDF2 rate? What RAM would 10,000 concurrent Argon2id (19 MiB) guesses need?

PBKDF2: your-rate x 10,000 (a GPU is faster still). Argon2id: 10,000 x 19 MiB = about 186 GB of RAM just to hold arrays — the economic argument (Demo 3 memory column).

Q8. Verification took ~the same time for correct and wrong passwords. Why does this matter?

Fixed work factor: no timing oracle on password or parameters; comparing the full tag avoids early-exit leaks (Simulation Verify tab note).
